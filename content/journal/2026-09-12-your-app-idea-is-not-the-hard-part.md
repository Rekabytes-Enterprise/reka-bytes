There's a pattern we see constantly with founders bringing ideas to us, and it
shows up even more when they try to build with AI on their own: the idea is
great, the conversation is energetic — and three weeks later the thing being
built is not the thing that was described.

Not because anyone did it wrong. Because the description was never finished.

## The three questions every spec answers

A product requirements document sounds bureaucratic. It's not — it's the
answer to three questions, written down before anyone builds:

1. **What must it do?** The list of things a user can accomplish. Every one of
   them, including the boring ones: "the user can reset their password,"
   "the admin can see all orders, not just their own." Boring is where teams
   get ambushed.
2. **What must it never do?** The quiet half of the list. A checkout that
   double-charges, a search that leaks someone else's data, a form that
   accepts a payment with an expired card and then silently fails. You can
   only avoid these if you imagine them first — that's what a requirements
   document is for.
3. **How do we know it's done?** Not vibes. A checklist you can run: click
   here, this should happen; do that badly, and this should be the error. If
   "done" is a feeling, you'll never have it.

## Why this matters more now, not less

Before AI, a vague idea was a small risk — a developer would ask questions as
they built, and friction forced clarity eventually.

With AI, you can produce software faster than you can produce requirements, and
the model will confidently fill every gap you left with _something_. It fills
them with the average of everything it's seen, which is rarely what you
actually wanted. The code isn't the bottleneck anymore. The specification is.

This is also exactly why our own academy teaches spec-writing first. Vibe
coding is a real skill; it's just incomplete without knowing what you're
vibing _for_.

## The cheapest version

If you're starting from zero, do this: write the three lists above for your
idea in one page. Ugly, bullet-pointed, no jargon. Hand that to anyone — a
freelancer, an agency, an AI agent — and you'll get three radically different
first drafts, and you'll know which one fits.
